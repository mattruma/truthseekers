# Logical Fallacies

A **logical fallacy** is an error in reasoning that weakens an argument.

> **Important:** Identifying a fallacy does **not** automatically prove that a conclusion is false. It shows that a particular argument does not adequately support its conclusion.

## 📌Avoiding the Question

These fallacies avoid dealing directly with the argument or evidence under consideration.

### ⚠️Ad Hominem

#### Description

Attacks the person making an argument rather than addressing the argument itself.

#### Example

> We shouldn't take Sarah's argument about tax policy seriously. She barely graduated college.

#### Explanation

Sarah's educational background does not determine whether her argument about tax policy is correct.

The argument should be evaluated based on its reasoning and evidence, not Sarah's personal characteristics.

### ⚠️Straw Man

#### Description

Misrepresents or exaggerates someone's argument so that a weaker version of it can be attacked more easily.

#### Example

> **Alex:** I think we should reduce military spending.
>
> **Jordan:** So you want us to get rid of the military and leave the country defenseless.

#### Explanation

Alex argued for reducing military spending, not eliminating the military.

Jordan has replaced Alex's actual position with a more extreme position and attacked that instead.

### ⚠️Red Herring

#### Description

Introduces an irrelevant issue that distracts attention from the original question or argument.

#### Example

> **Alex:** Why did the city spend twice the approved amount on the new building?
>
> **Jordan:** What we should really be talking about is how hard our city employees work.

#### Explanation

The dedication of city employees may be worth discussing, but it does not answer the question about the building's cost.

The new subject distracts from the issue that was actually raised.

### ⚠️Tu Quoque ("You Too")

#### Description

Responds to an argument by accusing the person making it of hypocrisy instead of addressing the argument.

#### Example

> **Parent:** Smoking is dangerous to your health.
>
> **Teenager:** You smoked when you were my age, so you can't tell me smoking is dangerous.

#### Explanation

The parent's past behavior may reveal inconsistency, but it does not establish that the claim about smoking is false.

A hypocrite can still make a true statement.

### ⚠️Appeal to Ridicule

#### Description

Dismisses an argument by making it appear foolish or absurd rather than showing why it is false.

#### Example

> You actually think we should spend money exploring Mars? That's ridiculous. We can barely fix the potholes here.

#### Explanation

Calling the proposal ridiculous does not demonstrate that space exploration is unjustified.

The proposal must be evaluated by considering its reasons, costs, benefits, and alternatives.

## 📌Making Assumptions

These fallacies reach conclusions by assuming something that has not been adequately established.

### ⚠️Begging the Question

#### Description

Assumes as a premise something that is essentially the same as the conclusion being argued for.

#### Example

> Capital punishment is morally wrong because the government should never have the right to execute people.

#### Explanation

The premise assumes the central point that needs to be established: that government execution is morally impermissible.

The argument needs an independent reason for believing that the government should never have that authority.

### ⚠️Circular Reasoning

#### Description

Uses the conclusion of an argument, directly or indirectly, as evidence for that same conclusion.

#### Example

> This newspaper is trustworthy because it publishes reliable information.
>
> We know its information is reliable because it comes from a trustworthy newspaper.

#### Explanation

Neither claim provides independent support for the other.

The reasoning simply moves in a circle rather than providing evidence that establishes the newspaper's reliability.

### ⚠️False Dilemma

#### Description

Presents only two choices when additional reasonable possibilities exist.

#### Example

> Either you support this new security law or you don't care about public safety.

#### Explanation

Someone could care deeply about public safety while believing that this particular law is ineffective, unnecessary, or harmful.

The argument incorrectly assumes that only two positions are possible.

### ⚠️Slippery Slope

#### Description

Claims that one action will lead to a series of increasingly extreme consequences without sufficient evidence that the chain of events will occur.

#### Example

> If students are allowed to redo one assignment, soon they'll expect to redo every assignment, and eventually grades will become meaningless.

#### Explanation

Allowing one assignment to be redone does not necessarily lead to unlimited retakes or meaningless grades.

The argument must establish why each step in the proposed chain of consequences is reasonably likely.

### ⚠️Loaded Question

#### Description

Asks a question that contains an assumption the person answering it has not accepted.

#### Example

> Have you stopped cheating on your exams?

#### Explanation

Answering either "yes" or "no" appears to accept the assumption that the person has been cheating.

The assumption must first be established before the question can fairly be asked in this form.

### ⚠️Post Hoc (False Cause)

#### Description

Assumes that because one event happened before another event, the first event caused the second.

#### Example

> I wore my new shoes during the game and our team won. These must be my lucky shoes.

#### Explanation

The victory occurred after the person wore the shoes, but sequence alone does not establish causation.

Other factors could explain the team's victory.

### ⚠️Appeal to Ignorance

#### Description

Claims something is true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.

#### Example

> No one has proved that aliens have never visited Earth, so aliens must have visited Earth.

#### Explanation

A lack of evidence against a claim does not ordinarily establish that the claim is true.

The person making the claim still needs positive evidence supporting it.

### ⚠️Composition

#### Description

Assumes that what is true of individual parts must also be true of the whole.

#### Example

> Every player on this basketball team is an excellent individual player, so this must be an excellent team.

#### Explanation

Excellent individual players do not necessarily work well together as a team.

Characteristics of individual parts do not always transfer to the whole.

### ⚠️Division

#### Description

Assumes that what is true of a whole must also be true of each individual part.

#### Example

> That corporation is extremely wealthy, so everyone who works there must be wealthy.

#### Explanation

A wealthy corporation can employ people with very different levels of income and wealth.

A characteristic of the whole does not necessarily belong to every individual part.

## 📌Statistical Fallacies

These fallacies misuse samples, probabilities, correlations, or other forms of numerical evidence.

### ⚠️Hasty Generalization

#### Description

Draws a broad conclusion from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative.

#### Example

> Two employees from that company were rude to me. That company must hire terrible people.

#### Explanation

Two employees are not necessarily representative of everyone employed by the company.

A broader conclusion requires a sufficiently large and representative sample.

### ⚠️Correlation Does Not Imply Causation

#### Description

Assumes that because two things occur together, one must cause the other.

#### Example

> Cities with more police officers tend to have more crime. Therefore, having more police officers causes more crime.

#### Explanation

The correlation alone does not establish which factor causes the other, or whether a third factor explains both.

For example, cities with more crime may hire more police officers in response to the crime.

### ⚠️Cherry Picking

#### Description

Selects evidence that supports a conclusion while ignoring relevant evidence that challenges it.

#### Example

> This medical treatment clearly works. Here are three patients who improved after receiving it.

#### Explanation

Those three cases could be relevant, but they cannot establish the treatment's effectiveness if hundreds of unsuccessful cases are being ignored.

Evidence should be evaluated as a whole rather than selecting only favorable results.

### ⚠️Anecdotal Fallacy

#### Description

Uses a personal experience or isolated example as if it outweighs broader and more systematic evidence.

#### Example

> Studies say texting while driving increases the risk of an accident, but I've texted while driving many times and I've never crashed.

#### Explanation

One person's experience does not establish the overall level of risk.

Reliable statistical evidence based on many cases is generally stronger evidence about risk than an isolated personal experience.

### ⚠️Biased Sample

#### Description

Draws a conclusion about a population using a sample that does not adequately represent that population.

#### Example

> An online poll of our website's readers found that 90% of people support our position. Therefore, 90% of Americans support it.

#### Explanation

The website's readers may differ significantly from the general population.

A sample must adequately represent the population before its results can reasonably be generalized to that population.

### ⚠️Base Rate Neglect

#### Description

Ignores important information about how common something is when evaluating the probability of a particular case.

#### Example

> A disease test is 99% accurate, and I tested positive. Therefore, there is a 99% chance I have the disease.

#### Explanation

The probability also depends on factors such as how common the disease is and the test's false-positive and false-negative rates.

A highly accurate test can still produce a significant proportion of false positives when testing for a very rare condition.

## 📌Propaganda

These techniques attempt to persuade primarily through emotion, social pressure, association, or presentation rather than through adequate evidence and reasoning.

> **Note:** Not every propaganda technique is automatically a logical fallacy. Some are better understood as persuasive techniques that can be used to distract from or substitute for a sound argument.

### ⚠️Appeal to Popularity (Bandwagon)

#### Description

Claims or implies that something must be true, good, or correct because many people believe or support it.

#### Example

> Millions of people believe in astrology, so there must be something to it.

#### Explanation

The number of people who believe a claim does not determine whether the claim is true.

Popular beliefs can be correct or incorrect. The relevant question is whether adequate evidence supports the belief.

### ⚠️Appeal to Emotion

#### Description

Attempts to establish a conclusion primarily by provoking an emotional response rather than providing relevant reasons or evidence.

#### Example

> You should find the defendant innocent. Imagine how devastated his children will be if he goes to prison.

#### Explanation

The effect of imprisonment on the defendant's children may be emotionally significant, but it does not establish whether the defendant committed the crime.

The conclusion should be based on evidence relevant to guilt or innocence.

### ⚠️Appeal to Fear

#### Description

Attempts to persuade by emphasizing frightening consequences rather than establishing that the claim itself is true.

#### Example

> If you don't support this policy, criminals will take over our neighborhoods and none of us will be safe.

#### Explanation

A possible negative consequence can be relevant if there is evidence showing that it is likely.

Fear becomes fallacious when the emotional reaction substitutes for evidence connecting the proposed action to the feared outcome.

### ⚠️Appeal to Authority

#### Description

Treats someone's status or authority as sufficient reason to accept a claim, especially when the person lacks relevant expertise or the claim requires additional evidence.

#### Example

> A famous physicist says this diet prevents cancer, so the diet must work.

#### Explanation

Expertise in physics does not make someone an expert in nutrition, oncology, or medical research.

The relevant evidence and the authority's expertise should be evaluated rather than accepting the claim merely because an impressive person made it.

### ⚠️Appeal to Tradition

#### Description

Claims that a belief or practice is correct or superior simply because it has existed for a long time.

#### Example

> We've always done it this way, so this must be the best way to do it.

#### Explanation

A tradition may have good reasons behind it, but its age alone does not establish that it is correct or optimal.

The practice should be evaluated based on its merits.

### ⚠️Appeal to Novelty

#### Description

Claims that something is better or more correct simply because it is new.

#### Example

> This is the newest educational theory, so it must be better than traditional approaches.

#### Explanation

New ideas can represent genuine improvements, but being new does not establish that they are better.

The new approach still needs evidence demonstrating its advantages.

### ⚠️Genetic Fallacy

#### Description

Judges a claim based primarily on its source or origin rather than evaluating the claim itself.

#### Example

> That research was first promoted by a political activist, so it can't be scientifically valid.

#### Explanation

The origin of a claim may give us reasons to investigate it carefully, but it does not by itself establish whether the claim is true or false.

The evidence supporting the claim must still be evaluated.

### ⚠️Middle Ground Fallacy

#### Description

Assumes that the correct position must be a compromise somewhere between two opposing positions.

#### Example

> One person says the Earth is spherical and another says it's flat. The truth must be somewhere in between.

#### Explanation

Compromise can be useful when resolving disagreements, but truth is not determined by averaging opposing claims.

One position may be substantially better supported by the evidence than the other.

## 📌Formal vs. Informal Fallacies

It can be helpful to distinguish between **formal** and **informal** fallacies.

### Formal Fallacy

A **formal fallacy** occurs when the logical structure of an argument is invalid.

For example:

> 1. If it rains, the street will be wet.
> 2. The street is wet.
> 3. Therefore, it rained.

This commits the fallacy of **affirming the consequent**.

The street could be wet for another reason.

### Informal Fallacy

An **informal fallacy** usually involves problems with relevance, evidence, ambiguity, or unjustified assumptions.

For example:

> "His argument about economics is wrong because he's an idiot."

Whether the person is intelligent has no bearing on whether the economic argument is sound.

## 📌A Fallacy Does Not Necessarily Make the Conclusion False

This distinction is extremely important.

Consider:

> 1. Everyone believes the Earth is spherical.
> 2. Therefore, the Earth is spherical.

This commits an **appeal to popularity**.

But the conclusion is still true.

The problem is that the **argument does not establish the conclusion**.

In other words:

**Bad argument ≠ false conclusion**

A person may reach the correct conclusion for the wrong reasons.

## 📌Questions for Evaluating an Argument

Rather than simply trying to memorize dozens of fallacy names, ask:

1. **Does the conclusion actually follow from the premises?**
2. **Are the premises supported by adequate evidence?**
3. **Is the evidence relevant to the conclusion?**
4. **Has the argument ignored reasonable alternatives or contrary evidence?**

These questions help expose many common errors in reasoning even when you cannot remember the formal name of the fallacy.

## 📌A Caution About Calling Something a Fallacy

Not every **appeal to authority**, **appeal to emotion**, **appeal to consequences**, or similar argument is automatically fallacious.

For example:

> "The National Weather Service predicts severe thunderstorms this afternoon, so we should probably postpone the outdoor event."

This relies on an authority, but that does not make the reasoning fallacious. The authority has relevant expertise and evidence.

Compare:

> "A famous actor says severe thunderstorms aren't dangerous, so there's nothing to worry about."

Here, the person's fame provides no relevant expertise.

The important question is therefore not simply:

> **"Can I identify a fallacy?"**

but:

> **"Does the evidence offered actually justify the conclusion?"**
