# Definitions

Clear discussion requires clear language. 

Many words used when discussing truth are closely related and are sometimes used as synonyms in everyday conversation. However, they often have important differences.

This page provides working definitions for terms we may use in Truth Seekers Club discussions. 

These definitions are intended to help us understand one another, not to settle every philosophical debate about the terms.

## 📖 Reality

**Reality is everything that actually exists or occurs. Simply put, reality is the way things actually are.**

Reality does not depend upon what any particular person believes, perceives, or says about it.

### Related Terms

- **Existence** — The state of actually being.
- **Actuality** — What actually exists or occurs, as opposed to what is merely possible or imagined.
- **The real world** — An informal expression referring to reality as it actually exists.

### Example

> There are six apples in the basket.

If there actually are six apples, that is part of reality regardless of whether anyone has counted them correctly.

## 📖 Truth

**Truth is an accurate description of reality. A claim is true when it corresponds to reality as it actually is, including any relevant conditions such as time, place, and perspective.**

Truth should be distinguished from what someone **believes** to be true.

### Related Terms

- **True** — Accurately corresponds to reality.
- **Correct** — Often used informally to mean that a claim or answer is true.
- **Accurate** — Represents reality correctly.
- **Fact** — Often used as a synonym for truth in ordinary conversation, although the terms can be distinguished more precisely.

### Example

> There are six apples in the basket.

If there actually are six apples, the statement is **true**.

### Important Distinction

**Truth and belief are not the same thing.** 

A person can sincerely believe something that is false, just as a person can doubt something that is true.

## 📖 Fact

**A fact is a particular feature, event, condition, or state of reality that is actually the case.**

In everyday language, *fact* and *truth* are often used interchangeably. For our discussions, it can be useful to distinguish them.

### Related Terms

- **Truth** — Often used interchangeably with fact.
- **State of affairs** — A philosophical term for a particular way things are.
- **Actuality** — Something that is actually the case.
- **Reality** — The broader category of everything that actually exists or occurs.

### Example

> The basket contains six apples.

The **fact** is that the basket contains six apples. 

The **claim** "There are six apples in the basket" is **true** because it accurately describes that fact.

### Important Distinction

**A fact is something about reality that is actually the case. Truth describes the relationship between a claim and reality.** 

In ordinary conversation, however, saying "That's a fact" and "That's true" often communicates essentially the same thing.

## 📖 Claim

**A claim is a statement that asserts something about reality and can therefore potentially be evaluated as true or false.**

Making a claim does not establish that the claim is true.

### Related Terms

- **Statement** — Something expressed in words; not every statement necessarily makes a truth claim.
- **Assertion** — A statement presented as true.
- **Proposition** — In philosophy and logic, roughly the content of a statement that can be true or false.
- **Contention** — A claim, particularly one involved in a disagreement or argument.

### Example

> There are six apples in the basket.

This is a claim. Whether the claim is **true or false** depends upon reality.

## 📖 Belief

**A belief is something a person accepts or regards as true.**

A belief describes what someone thinks is true, not necessarily what actually is true.

### Related Terms

- **Opinion** — Often used interchangeably with belief, although opinions frequently involve judgments, evaluations, or preferences.
- **Conviction** — A strongly held belief.
- **View** — A person's belief or judgment about something.
- **Position** — A belief or conclusion someone holds regarding an issue.
- **Assumption** — Something accepted as true, sometimes without sufficient evidence.

### Example

> I believe there are six apples in the basket.

The speaker has expressed a belief. The belief may be **true or false**.

### Important Distinction

**The strength or sincerity of a belief does not determine whether it is true.**

## 📖 Opinion

**An opinion is a judgment, evaluation, preference, or belief held by a person.**

*Opinion* and *belief* are frequently used as synonyms, but distinguishing them can be useful.

### Related Terms

- **Belief** — Something accepted as true.
- **View** — A person's way of thinking about something.
- **Judgment** — A conclusion or evaluation.
- **Preference** — What someone favors or likes.
- **Assessment** — An evaluation based on some standard or evidence.

### Example

> Chocolate ice cream tastes better than vanilla.

This expresses a personal preference.

> Chocolate ice cream contains more calories than vanilla ice cream.

That may be called an opinion in casual conversation, but it actually contains a **factual claim** that can be investigated.

### Important Distinction

**Calling something an opinion does not automatically mean that it cannot be true or false.** Some opinions express personal preferences. Others contain claims about reality that can be tested.

## 📖 Perspective

**Perspective is the point of view or reference point from which someone experiences, observes, or understands reality.**

Different perspectives do not necessarily imply different truths.

### Related Terms

- **Point of view** — The position from which something is considered.
- **Viewpoint** — A particular way of viewing an issue.
- **Frame of reference** — The position or context relative to which something is observed.
- **Standpoint** — A person's position or perspective concerning something.

### Example

> Jane is sitting to my right.

> Jane is sitting to John's left.

Both statements can be true if the speakers occupy different positions. They describe the same reality from different reference points.

### Important Distinction

**Different perspectives can describe different aspects of the same reality without creating different realities.**

## 📖 Perception

**Perception is how reality appears to us through our senses and experiences.**

Our perceptions provide important information about reality, but they are not infallible.

### Related Terms

- **Observation** — Something noticed through the senses or measurement.
- **Experience** — What someone personally encounters.
- **Impression** — How something initially appears or seems.
- **Appearance** — How something seems to an observer.

### Example

> The stick appears bent when partially submerged in water.

The perception is real: the stick really does appear bent to the observer. But the conclusion that the stick itself is physically bent may be false.

### Important Distinction

**Our perception of reality and reality itself are not necessarily identical.**

## 📖 Evidence

**Evidence is information that gives us reason to accept or reject a claim.**

Evidence helps us determine whether our beliefs and claims accurately correspond to reality.

### Related Terms

- **Proof** — Evidence or reasoning sufficient to establish a conclusion, although the required standard varies by context.
- **Data** — Collected information that may serve as evidence.
- **Observation** — Something observed that may provide evidence.
- **Testimony** — Information provided by another person.
- **Reason** — A consideration supporting or opposing a conclusion.
- **Support** — Evidence or reasoning that strengthens a claim.

### Example

> It rained last night.

Possible evidence might include rainfall measurements, wet pavement, weather records, photographs, or eyewitness testimony.

### Important Distinction

**Evidence supports a claim; it is not necessarily the same thing as proof.** 

Evidence can be incomplete, misleading, misunderstood, or outweighed by stronger evidence.

## 📖 Knowledge

**Knowledge is, roughly speaking, a true belief that a person has adequate reason or justification to accept.**

The traditional philosophical account of knowledge is often described as **justified true belief**, although philosophers have identified important problems with treating that as a complete definition.

### Related Terms

- **Understanding** — Comprehension of something and often how or why it works.
- **Awareness** — Recognition or consciousness of something.
- **Certainty** — A very high degree of confidence or the absence of doubt.
- **Justified belief** — A belief supported by adequate reasons or evidence.

### Example

> There are six apples in the basket.

Someone believes there are six apples, carefully counts them, and there actually are six. The person has a **belief**, evidence supporting that belief, and a belief that is **true**.

### Important Distinction

**Knowledge and certainty are not necessarily the same thing.** 

We may have good reason to say we know something without claiming that there is absolutely no possibility that we could be mistaken.

## Commonly Confused Terms

Many of these terms are used interchangeably in everyday conversation.

They have important differences when discussing truth, knowledge, and evidence. 

The distinctions below can help us communicate more precisely and avoid disagreements that result simply from using the same words differently.

| Terms | Relationship | Important Difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Truth & Fact** | Often used as synonyms | A fact is something that is actually the case; truth describes whether a claim accurately corresponds to reality. |
| **Belief & Opinion** | Frequently overlap | A belief is something accepted as true; an opinion often involves judgment, evaluation, or preference. |
| **Perspective & Opinion** | Often confused | Perspective is the position from which something is viewed; opinion is a judgment or belief about it. |
| **Perception & Reality** | Closely connected | Perception is how reality appears to us; reality is how things actually are. |
| **Evidence & Proof** | Closely related | Evidence supports a conclusion; proof implies that the evidence or reasoning is sufficient to establish it. |
| **Knowledge & Belief** | Knowledge includes belief | Believing something does not mean that you know it. Knowledge also requires truth and appropriate justification or grounding. |
| **Truth & Certainty** | Often confused | Truth concerns reality; certainty concerns our confidence about what is true. |
| **Consensus & Truth** | Sometimes correlated | Agreement may provide evidence, but agreement itself does not make a claim true. |

## Working Principles

**Reality is what is.**

**Facts are things that are actually the case.**

**Truth accurately describes reality.**

**Claims attempt to describe reality.**

**Beliefs are what we accept as true.**

**Opinions express beliefs, judgments, evaluations, or preferences.**

**Perspectives are the positions from which we encounter reality.**

**Perceptions are how reality appears to us.**

**Evidence gives us reasons to accept or reject claims.**

**Knowledge requires more than simply believing something.**

The goal of truth-seeking is therefore not merely to ask:

> What do I believe?

but to ask:

> What is actually the case, and do I have good reason to believe that my beliefs and claims correspond to it?
